## Building

_511 commits · 48 PRs · 7 open · ~73 commits/day_

The fund repo has been around a while.  What happened this week is that I finally admitted real technology had grown inside it that had no business being there, and pulled it out into its own repo.  The site came apart the same way — two sites now instead of one, free to evolve independently rather than dragging each other along.

The tech side *reads* the fund's canonical data and cannot write it; every write path refuses and emits a typed proposal that has to land through a pull request instead.  When you split a system, the question isn't what goes where.  It's which side is allowed to write.

Started moving that seam from markdown to MCP — contract points an agent can *call*, rather than documents describing repo structure that every agent has to read and interpret correctly.

Introduced **Outcomes**: dated short-term commitments that specs and agent development hang off, so a spec has to name what it's for before it earns a place.

Built out the tech side's own site — manifesto, direction, where this is heading — and pointed the first outcome at a portco founder meeting: what has genuinely advanced in their stack.  Started giving each agent a defined role rather than overlapping ones, and shoring up what each is actually good at.

Almost every mistake I fixed this week was the same one in different clothes: my own system asserting something that was never true.  A decision that looked made and never was, a component the design implied existed with nothing producing it, a rule everyone was following that had never been agreed.  You act on your notes, not on reality — which makes notes that quietly overclaim more dangerous than notes with holes in them.  A hole sends you to go and look.

**Learning.**  Running several coding agents at once — across different tasks, on different machines — is harder than it looks, and the bottleneck isn't any agent's output.  It's me losing track of which one is mid-flight where, and re-reading my own work to find out.

**And the vampire is real.**  Indoors, at a screen, all day, is the default failure mode of this whole arrangement, and it degrades the work well before you notice it happening.  Get out.  Walk, ride, lift — the clearest thinking I did on the split, and the manifesto that came out of it, happened on a bike ride with my family, nowhere near a keyboard.

## Rooms I Was In

**[AI Safety Night SF](https://luma.com/acywrxdj)** (Aug 18) — five lightning talks from working researchers.  The reframe I keep chewing on: you can't make a model un-foolable, so you make *being fooled* non-catastrophic.  Authorization is the only layer that gets **stronger** as models improve.

**[Claude Code + OpenClaw, FounderCoHo](https://luma.com/livestream-8-19)** (Aug 19) — Nat Eliason demoing the personal-agent stack he actually runs his business on, including the parts that break.  His reliability complaint was the useful part: the gap between hobbyist automation and dependable autonomy is a *runtime* problem — durability, restart, state, permissions, escalation — and none of it is waiting on a better model.

**a16z infra dinner** (Aug 20) — a room of founders mingling, and me there as an operator rather than as anyone's representative.  First room I've walked into with no company behind my name.  My name tag still said Netflix.  Definitely felt a bit lost.  What did work: I ran prep out of the fund repo beforehand, so I knew something about who'd be there — and talking to people you already know something about skips the introductory lap entirely.
