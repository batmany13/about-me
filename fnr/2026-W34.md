I left Netflix at the end of the previous week.  I had a vague plan to ease in.  Instead I split a repo in half, which turns out to have been the right instinct: the first thing worth doing with a lot of unstructured freedom is to impose some structure on something.

## Building

**The split.**  I pulled the research and evaluation engine out of the fund's monorepo into its own repository — [Finding Alphas](https://github.com/batmany13/findingalphas) — and got it standalone in four days: marker-based root discovery replacing a genuinely embarrassing parent-directory-counting hack, a reference sweep across every live file, 627 tests green, CI on push and PR, and the website build passing route-parity against the pre-split baseline.

**The decision inside the split was the interesting part.**  The new repo *reads* the fund's canonical data; it cannot write it.  Every write path refuses, and emits a typed proposal that has to land through a pull request on the other side instead.  Without an explicit pointer to the fund checkout, reads report the lane unavailable rather than guessing.

That cost me most of a day I could have skipped, and it's the only reason the two repos are now independently reasonable rather than one repo living in two directories.  When you split a system, the question isn't what goes where.  It's which side is allowed to write.

**Research.**  A fresh v3 pass on [CocoIndex](https://cocoindex.io), and a docs-version register for DeepVista.  The generalization out of the second one: for a closed-source subject you can't diff the code, so you diff the docs across versions and treat *that* as the iteration axis.  Obvious in retrospect, which is the good kind.

**Things I got wrong.**  I backtested my own research-plan format across ten releases and it broke in five places — it had no way to express a `modify` verdict, and claims weren't first-class, so an assertion at risk had nowhere to live.  Separately: I'd been counting five emitters where there are four, and I finally recorded that a transport binding was *never chosen* rather than leaving the repo implying a decision I hadn't made.  Writing down "we never decided this" is underrated.

## Fund & Advisory

The other half of the split: the fund repo stops being an umbrella and becomes a pointer.  Fund-side workflows now emit typed requests instead of reaching into the external system directly.

I added an outcome layer — dated commitments the specs hang off, so "what is this deliverable actually *for*" has an answer that isn't vibes.  And I made coverage computable rather than asserted, which promptly surfaced a coverage failure in one of my own analysis runs.  Recorded it, ran the retro, folded it back into the spec.

Best thing I wrote all week was a commit message: *the finding had no consumer.*  A research result nobody downstream is waiting for isn't a finding.  It's a note.

## Rooms I Was In

**[AI Safety Night SF](https://luma.com/acywrxdj)** (Aug 18) — five lightning talks from working researchers.  The reframe I keep chewing on: you can't make a model un-foolable, so you make *being fooled* non-catastrophic.  Authorization is the only layer that gets **stronger** as models improve; anything depending on the model reliably telling instructions from data gets weaker.  Also: a guardrail can't be certified independently of the model it wraps — the same stack can improve one model's robustness by an order of magnitude and make another's worse.

**[Claude Code + OpenClaw, FounderCoHo](https://luma.com/livestream-8-19)** (Aug 19) — Nat Eliason demoing the personal-agent stack he actually runs his business on, live, including the parts that break. That gap — between hobbyist automation and dependable autonomy — is a runtime problem.  Durability, restart, state, schedule ownership, permissions, observability, escalation.  None of that is waiting on a better model.

## Top of Mind for Founders

**A number without a denominator isn't a measurement.**  In the same week I heard a 20–50× productivity claim with no baseline, output unit, time window, or task mix attached, and learned that a dozen published defenses claiming near-zero attack success got broken past 90% once someone attacked them adaptively.  Same disease, different rooms.  If you're quoting a multiple to your board or a benchmark to a customer, the useful question isn't "is it true" — it's "what would have to be defined for this to be checkable."  If you can't answer that, you have a slogan.

**What you refuse to delegate is your real statement about trust.**  The operator making the 20–50× claim still writes his own marketing copy, by hand, on principle — while agents research, edit, repurpose, and distribute around it.  That tension isn't hypocrisy, it's a boundary, and it's more informative than the multiple.  Worth asking yourself the same question directly: what have you kept? That's where you actually believe the judgment lives.

**Delegating to agents is a management problem you've already half-solved.**  Set intent, delegate, monitor, review, correct, escalate — that ladder transfers cleanly, and most people fumbling agent workflows are fumbling the delegation, not the prompt.  The one thing that doesn't transfer: a good direct report tells you when they're stuck.  An agent confidently does not.  So the monitoring rung has to carry weight it never had to carry with people.
