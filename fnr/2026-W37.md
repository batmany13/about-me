## Building

_465 commits · 43 PRs · 13 open · ~66 commits/day_

The store migration finished its middle: a provisional cutover, then the real one.  The public side now reads from the hosted database through a signed, password-gated proxy instead of from files, and caching took the slowest page from about seven seconds to under one.  Then the harder half — retiring the old store.  That turned out to be slower and more forensic than building the new one: the first pass had deleted files before checking which consumers still read them, so the repair restored everything, verified 798 deleted paths byte-for-byte against archived originals, and only then retired them.  Recovery got proved rather than asserted — a full restore of about 17,000 rows across 43 tables, verified against every journal head.

Then I tested it with real use cases rather than fixtures.  Building out the architecture loop took more use cases than I'd planned — several agents running against the live service, each surfacing its own bugs, addressed as they came.

I also reorganised the work into two things.  One is getting it running on real infrastructure, which is most of what's above.  The other is building out the product vision and implementing the next set of features.  Everything else was either closed or folded into one of those two.

**Learning.**  <!-- fnr:building_learning -->
A migration plan is something you discover, not something you write up front.  Mine went from fifteen steps to seventeen over about seventy revisions, and the iteration was the point.  An outcome with numbered steps is a good starting point because it gives discovery somewhere to land: each step turns up what actually needs fixing.  The risk runs the other way too.  Every discovery looks urgent, and chasing all of them is how an outcome never finishes.  So the structure grew a second list: anything found along the way that the outcome didn't need went to a follow-on, named and owned, instead of into the plan.

**Learning.**  <!-- fnr:fund_learning -->
A company shed an entire product line and the team that ran it.  The transaction isn't the interesting part; the willingness is.  A working customer base can belong to the company you used to be rather than the one you're becoming, and being able to let it go is a capability most teams don't have.
