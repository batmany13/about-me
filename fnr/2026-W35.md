# 2026-W35 — Aug 24–30, 2026

**The week I stopped having nine answers to the same question.**

## Building

_293 commits · 49 PRs · 9 open · ~42 commits/day_

Two things, and 68% of the week went into them.

The larger one was rebuilding how research accumulates. I had a pipeline that evaluated technologies, and its problem was that every pass started over — a subject I'd looked at in June, picked up again in August, began from nothing. Worse, different parts of the system used the same words to mean different depths, so "we've researched this" meant whatever the surface saying it happened to mean. That is now **one declared ladder, in one place, with a gate that fails anything defining its own vocabulary**. How much I actually know about a given technology is a single number a reader can trust, and a subject picked up next month carries its history rather than restarting.

The smaller one was worse before it was fixed: I had **nine documents that each answered "what should I work on," and each of them sounded right.** A roadmap, a focus file, several plans. None of them was wrong exactly, which is what made it expensive — I'd read whichever one I opened and get a defensible answer that didn't agree with the last defensible answer. That collapsed into a single direction surface with the old ones retired outright, and planning now reads what to do next from work that is actually committed rather than ranking whatever I happened to write down most recently.

**Learning.** The failure mode in both was the same and it isn't disorganisation — it's **plausibility**. A stale roadmap and a live one look identical. A shallow read and a deep one both say "researched." Nothing in either system was false; there were just several true-sounding answers to one question, and no rule about which one counted. The fix in both cases wasn't better documents, it was picking one place to hold the answer and building a gate that rejects the others — which costs something real, because it means deleting work that still reads well.

**And the vampire is still real.** Same lesson as last week and I didn't learn it fast enough: the clearest thinking about which nine documents to delete did not happen at the desk.

## Fund & Advisory

Nine founder and network conversations, and the work that came out of them wasn't evaluating anyone — it was giving conversations a home. Until this week a conversation had nowhere of its own to live: it either got folded into a company's evaluation, where **what a founder told me hardens into company fact simply by being restated**, or it was lost.

They are now dated records, separate from the evaluation, with the rule codified in six places because I'd already broken it once by hand.

**Learning.** Keeping *what someone said* apart from *what I now believe* is not bookkeeping — it is the difference between a claim with a source and a fact with none. Every time I collapsed the two, the founder's framing quietly became my analysis, and I couldn't reconstruct which parts I'd actually checked.

The other half is what expires. Company state keeps — still true next month. An intro I owe, a question queued before a call, a follow-up someone is waiting on: those go stale in days, and they were exactly the half getting dropped while three overlapping versions of the same conversation were preserved.

## Top of Mind for Founders

Two kinds of conversation right now, and they are not the same one. Some founders have already raised and are hunting for the wedge — the money is in the bank and the question is traction. Others have the ARR and are asking what the next stage looks like. They intermingle sometimes, where traction convinces someone it's time to raise so they can focus, but the mistake is assuming they always do.

Where they land in the same place is customers, customers, customers. Is it easy enough to use? Do people just *get it*? Are they clamouring for it? That's the product-led growth question, and the hard part is building that engine while the engine is still you. Founder energy powers all of it, which is fine right up until it isn't — first engineer, tenth hire, somewhere in there. Do what doesn't scale, then do what does, for the stage you're actually at.
